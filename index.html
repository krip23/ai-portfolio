Introduction

Description

This artifact covers the results of both assessments along with my honest take on my top strengths and growth areas in each category, my evaluation of both tools as professional instruments, and specific revisions I would make to improve them. The point was not to look good on paper — it was to actually figure out where I stand and what I need to work on going forward.

Objective
  • To honestly evaluate where I am strong and where I have real gaps as a leader in AI/ML and change management.
  • To understand what effective change leadership looks like specifically in an AI/ML context.
Process

I completed both assessment forms from start to finish, rating myself on each item using the 1 to 5 scale. Once I was done, I identified the three highest and three lowest scoring areas in each one. I then read both articles assigned for Part 1 — the CCL piece on successful change leadership and the Change Strategists piece on assessing change management skills. With all of that in front of me, I wrote the reflection: what the scores told me, what I actually think of the tools, and what I would change about them if I were designing them myself.

Self-assessment findings and evaluation

Top 3 Strengths: Stakeholder Communication, understanding how AI applies to business situations, and keeping up with new developments in the field. These reflect the work I do most consistently in my professional role — especially translating technical ideas into language that non-technical stakeholders can understand and act on.

Top 3 Growth Areas: Building structured training programs for AI adoption, scaling AI tools across larger organizations, and measuring whether AI projects are actually delivering value long term. The training gap is the one I find most important to address. Getting an AI solution working is a different skill than helping an organization sustain it, and I have not invested enough in the second one.

Assessment Findings — Change Management Skills

Top 3 Strengths: Communication Skills, Flexibility and Adaptability, and Continuous Learning. The adaptability score felt accurate — AI projects almost never go exactly as planned, and I have gotten used to adjusting without losing sight of the end goal.

Top 3 Growth Areas: Formal change management frameworks, Resilience under sustained pressure, and Conflict Resolution. I know how to manage change in practice but have not formally studied methods like Prosci's ADKAR model or Kotter's 8-Step Process. The conflict resolution gap is the most honest score on the page — I handle resistance well when I understand where it is coming from, but I tend to avoid difficult conversations when the source of pushback is unclear.

Evaluation of the Assessments

Both tools are a useful starting point, but they only work if the person completing them is willing to be honest. Someone who marks themselves a 5 on everything gets nothing useful out of it. The value comes entirely from the quality of the reflection behind the score. I also identified two gaps in the assessments themselves:

  • The AI/ML assessment does not include a category for AI Governance and Regulatory Literacy. As governments develop frameworks like the EU AI Act and NIST AI Risk Management Framework, this is becoming a required leadership skill, not an optional one.
  • The Change Management assessment does not include a category for Managing Change Fatigue — the exhaustion that builds when an organization is running several major AI initiatives at the same time. This is one of the most common reasons AI rollouts stall, and it deserves its own section.

Machine Learning vs. Deep Learning: Choosing the Right Approach

Description: A written report comparing two examples from the course lesson: house price prediction with linear regression (machine learning) and machine translation with Transformer models (deep learning). For each, the report identifies a real-world application, explains why the approach is suitable, and explains why the other approach is not. The full report is included below.

Objective:

I selected one ML example and one DL example from the lesson, found a real application of each, and judged both on the same five factors: data type, how features are defined, amount of data needed, computing cost, and explainability. I then wrote the report around those factors.

Full comparison report

Introduction Machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) both let computers learn patterns from data, but they suit different problems. In traditional ML, people choose the features (the data characteristics that matter) and an algorithm such as linear regression, a decision tree, or a support vector machine learns from them. Deep learning is a subset of ML that uses neural networks with many layers to discover useful features directly from raw data. DL usually needs far more data and computing power, while simpler ML models work well with smaller datasets and are faster to train and easier to explain. This report examines house price prediction using linear regression (ML) and machine translation using Transformer models (DL).

Example 1: Simple Machine Learning — House Price Prediction Real-world application. Property tax offices and appraisers commonly use regression-based mass appraisal models to estimate the value of many homes at once. Inputs are well-understood attributes such as square footage, bedrooms and bathrooms, lot size, age of the home, and neighborhood. Online home-value estimators use the same family of techniques to price homes against comparable sales.

Why machine learning is suitable. The features are already known, so the model does not need to discover them. The data is structured and moderate in size, which regression handles well. Explainability matters: a homeowner who disputes an assessment can be shown how each additional bathroom or square foot changes the estimate, and public agencies must be able to justify valuations. The model is also cheap and fast to retrain on standard hardware.

Why deep learning is not the best fit. A deep neural network would add complexity without clear benefit. With tabular data and well-chosen features, deep models often perform no better than simpler ones, they need more data and tuning to avoid overfitting, and their reasoning is hard to explain. That lack of transparency is a serious drawback when the result affects someone's tax bill or loan decision.

Example 2: Deep Learning — Machine Translation Real-world application. Google Translate moved from phrase-based statistical methods to neural machine translation in 2016 (Wu et al., 2016), and modern translation systems rely on Transformer architectures (Vaswani et al., 2017). These systems translate text between many languages in real time.

Why deep learning is suitable. The features cannot be hand-written: meaning depends on context, grammar, idiom, and word order, which differ across languages. Text is abundant, and deep networks thrive on very large datasets, learning from word patterns up to sentence meaning. Transformer models use attention to weigh how every word in a sentence relates to the others, so the word "bank" is handled differently near "river" than near "loan." The heavy computation is worthwhile because quality improves dramatically over rule- and feature-based methods.

Why simple machine learning is not suitable. A traditional ML model would require engineers to define features for language by hand, such as word counts or fixed phrases. Those features cannot capture long-range context, ambiguity, or the variety of sentence structures, so translations would be literal, awkward, and often wrong.

Comparison

  • Approach: House price prediction uses machine learning (linear regression); machine translation uses deep learning (Transformer).
  • Data type: structured, tabular vs. unstructured text.
  • Features: chosen by people vs. learned automatically.
  • Data needed: moderate vs. very large.
  • Computing cost: low vs. high.
  • Explainability: high vs. low.

Conclusion Deep learning is not automatically better than machine learning. When features are known, data is structured, and decisions must be explained, simple ML models like linear regression are efficient and trustworthy. When the input is complex and unstructured and the patterns are too intricate to define by hand, as with language, deep learning is the better tool despite its cost and opacity. Matching the method to the problem, rather than choosing the most advanced technique, is the key lesson for anyone leading AI-enabled work.

Value Proposition Unique Value: This artifact shows I can match an AI method to a problem instead of defaulting to the most advanced option, and explain the reasoning plainly. Knowing when a simpler model is the right call matters as much as knowing how to build a complex one. Relevance: Anyone leading AI adoption has to weigh cost, data needs, and whether stakeholders can trust the result. This comparison mirrors the questions a team asks before approving an AI project.

A History of Artificial Intelligence: Key Milestones Timeline

View ai history timeline ↓

Workshop 1 · Research Discussion · October 2026

Introduction

Discussion 1.3 asked me to research the history of AI and present the key milestones as a visual timeline. The assignment had three parts: the historical context from the 1950s, the AI Winters where progress stalled, and the recent breakthroughs tied to deep learning and new hardware. Putting it all together in one place helped me understand why the field developed the way it did — and why so many people were caught off guard by the pace of progress after 2012.

Description

A chronological timeline covering AI's development from 1950 to 2025. It walks through the major milestones, explains both AI Winters, identifies the key figures and organizations at each turning point, and shows why hardware improvements were just as important as new algorithms in driving the modern AI era. The goal was to produce something useful as a reference, not just as an assignment deliverable.

Objective
  • To research and map out the key moments in AI and machine learning history in a clear, accurate way.
  • To understand which figures, organizations, and decisions shaped the field — and which ones nearly ended it.
Process

I started with Putchuon's documentary overview on YouTube to get the full narrative arc, from Alan Turing to the current competition between OpenAI, Google, and others. I then used IEEE Computer Society's technical history to pin down accurate dates and terminology. Our World in Data's interactive charts helped me connect the growth in computing power to the jumps in AI capability. From those three sources I built the timeline, organizing milestones chronologically and adding short explanations for each one.

Tools and Technologies Used
  • YouTube — Putchuon documentary on AI history (2024)
  • IEEE Computer Society — technical history of AI
  • Our World in Data — interactive data on AI trajectory
  • Canva — for visual timeline design
  • Brightspace — for discussion submission

AI history timeline

  • 1950The Turing Test — Alan Turing asks "Can machines think?" and proposes the Imitation Game as a benchmark for machine intelligence.
  • 1956Dartmouth Conference — John McCarthy and Marvin Minsky coin the term "Artificial Intelligence." The official birth of AI as an academic field.
  • 1957–65Early Optimism — Frank Rosenblatt builds the Perceptron, the first neural network model. ELIZA demonstrates early natural language processing. Researchers predict human-level AI within 20 years.
  • 1974–80AI Winter I — Funding collapses. The Lighthill Report criticizes AI research. DARPA cuts funding. Hardware cannot support the theories being developed.
  • 1980–87Expert Systems Boom — Rule-based expert systems like XCON generate commercial excitement. Japan launches the Fifth Generation Computer Project.
  • 1987–93AI Winter II — Expert systems prove brittle and expensive. The Lisp machine market collapses. DARPA pulls funding again. The field nearly dies commercially.
  • 1997Deep Blue Beats Kasparov — IBM's Deep Blue defeats world chess champion Garry Kasparov for the first time — a cultural and technical milestone.
  • 2006–09Deep Learning Foundation — Geoffrey Hinton's team revives neural networks through deep belief networks. The groundwork for modern AI is laid quietly.
  • 2012The AlexNet Moment — AlexNet cuts ImageNet error rates by 10 points in one year. Nvidia GPUs make it possible. The modern AI era begins here.
  • 2016AlphaGo — Google DeepMind defeats world Go champion Lee Sedol 4–1 using reinforcement learning — intuition-like pattern recognition, not brute force.
  • 2017Transformer Architecture — Google publishes "Attention Is All You Need," creating the foundation of every major language model today.
  • 2022ChatGPT — OpenAI launches ChatGPT. Reaches 100 million users in two months — the fastest-growing consumer application in history.
  • 2023–25The Arms Race — OpenAI, Google, Meta, and Anthropic compete across multimodal AI, reasoning models, and AI agents.
Value Proposition
Unique Value

This timeline is designed to be useful not just as an assignment deliverable but as a reference resource. Someone new to AI/ML can use it to quickly understand the field's trajectory — the false starts, the winters, and what actually changed in 2012. That kind of context matters for making sense of where the field is going.

Relevance

Leaders and teams working with AI today benefit from understanding why the field developed the way it did. Knowing about the AI Winters helps explain why skepticism around AI can be reasonable, and knowing about AlexNet helps explain why 2012 was such a turning point. That background makes for better strategic decisions.
